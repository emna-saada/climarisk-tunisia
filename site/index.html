When the climate shifts, credit risk shifts with it.

A study that ties each loan in a bank portfolio to the ground it stands on, and measures how heat, drought, floods and wildfire change its risk today, in 2030 and in 2050.

Emna Saada · Geoinformatics engineering, MSE, Université de la Manouba

The portfolio is synthetic. No client or bank data is used; the figures illustrate the method.

How the study works

01 · The question

Banks lend against land, buildings and harvests. The climate is changing all three.

Tunisian banks finance cereal and olive farms, hotels on the coast, phosphate plants and housing. Each of these assets sits somewhere specific, and each place faces its own mix of heat, drought, flooding and fire. Yet credit files rarely record that link in a form a risk committee can use.

Supervisors are moving. The question this study asks is practical: if a bank knew where its loans are, how much of its book would sit in harm's way, and what would that do to expected loss?

  • 2022Basel Committee publishes 18 principles for managing climate-related financial risks.
  • 2024NGFS releases Phase V of its long-term climate scenarios.
  • 2025Basel Committee issues a voluntary framework for climate disclosure; EBA guidelines on ESG risks follow.
  • 2026The Central Bank of Tunisia launches a 2026–2030 roadmap on green finance and climate risk (press reports).

02 · The method

From pixels to probability of default, in four steps.

The logic follows the IPCC definition of risk: hazard, exposure and vulnerability. Each step is a module in the open-source code, and every coefficient sits in one configuration file.

Sh = (xh − ah) / (bh − ah)Each hazard indicator scaled to 0–1 with fixed physical bounds
C = Σ Vs,h Sh / Σ Vs,hWeighted by the sensitivity of the borrower's sector
PD′ = PD · (1 + βs·C)Probability of default rises with the composite score
EL = PD′ · LGD′ · EADFlood depth also raises LGD for land and property collateral

05 · Robustness

The credit assumption matters more than the climate model.

Halving or doubling the PD elasticity β moves the result by about −49 % to +98 %. The spread across climate models is only −10 % to +18 %. Calibrating β on defaults observed after Tunisian droughts and floods would narrow the uncertainty far more than adding climate models.

One result looks odd at first: a heavier flood weight slightly lowers the loss. Flood scores are low for most loans, and the composite score is a weighted average, so more weight on flood pulls it down.

The two drought indicators disagree on where drought bites. physrisk's SPEI is relative to local climate and flags the wetter north; the dry-spell measure used here is absolute and flags the arid south. Both show drought worsening at every location.

06 · Limits and next steps

What this study can and cannot say.

  • Synthetic portfolio. Sector shares, PDs and LGDs are documented assumptions, not central bank statistics.
  • Resolution. Climate data on a 25 km grid; flood maps at about 1 km miss urban run-off and small wadis.
  • Short windows. Five models and three years per horizon: the 2030 rainfall signal is mostly year-to-year noise.
  • Flood projections. Aqueduct uses CMIP5 and an observation-driven baseline, so flood change over time is not like-for-like.
  • Uncalibrated credit link. β, γ and the sector weights are illustrative.
  • Physical risk only. No transition risk. The NGFS Phase V GDP-damage function (Kotz et al., 2024, retracted) is not used.
